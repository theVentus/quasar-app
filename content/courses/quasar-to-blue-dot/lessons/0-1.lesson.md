TITLE: Time is distance
ABSTRACT: Satellite positioning turns measured travel times into distances. This lesson fixes the units, introduces the ranging equation and its error form, and shows why metre-level positions demand nanosecond timing.
===BODY===
## Intuition
In a thunderstorm you count the seconds between the flash and the thunder. Sound covers roughly a kilometre every three seconds, so a delay becomes a distance. Satellite positioning plays the same game with radio waves travelling at the speed of light. The difficulty lies entirely in the size of the numbers: the delays are tens of milliseconds, and they must be measured to within a few nanoseconds.

> **Remark (Where the analogy breaks).** With thunder you know when the flash happened because you saw it. A phone never sees the moment a satellite transmits; it has to infer it from the signal itself, and its own clock is poor. That gap is the subject of Modules 2 and 3.

## The defining constant
> **Definition (Speed of light).** The speed of light in vacuum, $c$, is exactly $299\,792\,458$ m/s. Since 1983 the metre has been defined as the distance light travels in vacuum in $1/299\,792\,458$ of a second [1, 2].

So $c$ is not a measured approximation; the unit of length is built from it. The second, in turn, is defined by fixing the frequency of a particular transition in the caesium-133 atom at $9\,192\,631\,770$ Hz [3]. That is the same physics that runs the clocks on the satellites (Module 4). In satellite positioning, ==distance is literally time multiplied by $c$==.

## Powers of ten
The numbers in this course run from picoseconds to tens of thousands of kilometres, so we write them as a number between 1 and 10 multiplied by a power of ten.

| Prefix | Symbol | Factor |
|---|---|---|
| kilo | k | $10^{3}$ |
| mega | M | $10^{6}$ |
| giga | G | $10^{9}$ |
| milli | m | $10^{-3}$ |
| micro | μ | $10^{-6}$ |
| nano | n | $10^{-9}$ |
| pico | p | $10^{-12}$ |

Two rules do almost all the work. To multiply, add the exponents: $10^{a}\times10^{b}=10^{a+b}$. To divide, subtract them: $10^{a}/10^{b}=10^{a-b}$. For example, $(3\times10^{8})\times10^{-9}=3\times10^{-1}$.

```quiz
Q: Which of these is the same length of time as $300$ ns?
- [ ] $3\times10^{-9}$ s
- [x] $3\times10^{-7}$ s
- [ ] $3\times10^{-5}$ s
- [ ] $0.3$ ms
> Nano means $10^{-9}$, so $300\ \mathrm{ns}=3\times10^{2}\times10^{-9}\ \mathrm{s}=3\times10^{-7}$ s: add the exponents, $2+(-9)=-7$. The last option is a thousand times too long, since $0.3\ \mathrm{ms}=3\times10^{-4}$ s.
```

## The ranging equation
Speed is distance per unit time, $c=d/t$. Multiplying both sides by $t$ gives
\begin{equation}
d = c\,t ,
\end{equation}
where $d$ is the distance in metres and $t$ the travel time in seconds. If the measured time is wrong by $\Delta t$ (read "delta t", the error in $t$), the distance is wrong by
\begin{equation}
\Delta d = c\,\Delta t .
\end{equation}
The units check: $(\mathrm{m/s})\times\mathrm{s}=\mathrm{m}$. Because $c$ is enormous, ==small timing errors become large distance errors==, and the relationship is strictly proportional: double the timing error and you double the range error.

Equation (1) is exact only under three assumptions: the signal travels in a straight line, through vacuum, and both ends agree on what time it is. Satellite positioning breaks all three. Buildings reflect signals and the atmosphere slows them (Module 7), and clocks in orbit run at a different rate from clocks on the ground (Module 6). Much of this course is a sequence of corrections to equation (1).

```quiz
Q: A signal reflects off a glass tower before it reaches your phone. Which assumption behind $d=c\,t$ does this break?
- [x] The signal travels in a straight line
- [ ] The signal travels through vacuum
- [ ] Both ends agree on what time it is
> The reflection lengthens the path, so the measured travel time corresponds to a distance longer than the straight line to the satellite. The atmosphere breaks the vacuum assumption, and clock errors break the third.
```

> **Key idea.** Light travels about 30 cm in one nanosecond: $3\times10^{8}\ \mathrm{m/s}\times10^{-9}\ \mathrm{s}=0.3\ \mathrm{m}$.

## Worked examples
> **Example (A 100 ns error).** A timing error of 100 ns gives $\Delta d = 0.3\ \mathrm{m/ns}\times100\ \mathrm{ns}=30$ m. A tenth of a microsecond is enough to put you on the wrong side of a street.

> **Example (Travel time from overhead).** GPS satellites orbit at an altitude of about 20,200 km [4]. For a satellite directly overhead, $t=d/c=2.02\times10^{7}\ \mathrm{m}\,/\,3.00\times10^{8}\ \mathrm{m/s}\approx6.7\times10^{-2}\ \mathrm{s}=67$ ms.

How precisely must that 67 ms interval be measured? Attempt each step before revealing it.

```steps
title: The precision needed for a one-metre range
---
A one-metre range error corresponds to a timing error of $$\Delta t=\frac{\Delta d}{c}=\frac{1\ \mathrm{m}}{3.00\times10^{8}\ \mathrm{m/s}}\approx3.3\ \mathrm{ns}.$$
---
The interval being measured is the travel time itself, $t\approx6.7\times10^{-2}$ s.
---
The relative precision required is their ratio, $$\frac{\Delta t}{t}=\frac{3.3\times10^{-9}\ \mathrm{s}}{6.7\times10^{-2}\ \mathrm{s}}\approx5\times10^{-8},$$ about one part in $2\times10^{7}$. Module 3 explains how a receiver achieves this.
```

> **Example (Clocks that run at the wrong rate).** A clock whose frequency is off by a fraction $y$ (a pure number, often quoted in parts per million or per billion) drifts in proportion to the elapsed time $T$:
> \begin{equation}
> \Delta t = y\,T .
> \end{equation}
> Relativity makes a GPS satellite clock gain about 38 μs per day relative to clocks on the ground [5]. With $T=86\,400$ s, $y=38\times10^{-6}/86\,400\approx4.4\times10^{-10}$. One day's gain corresponds to a range of $c\,\Delta t=3.00\times10^{8}\times3.8\times10^{-5}\approx1.1\times10^{4}$ m, about 11 km.

This is the "GPS would drift by about 10 km a day" figure that popular accounts quote. Module 6 tests whether that is what would actually happen. The real failure is subtler, for reasons Exercise 4 begins to uncover.

## Deeper theory
Because $c$ is fixed by definition, measuring a length reduces to measuring a time. That is why national time laboratories, navigation systems and length standards are so closely linked: the same atomic clocks underpin all three.

Strictly, $c$ is the speed in vacuum. In a medium, a wave travels at $v=c/n$, where $n$ is the refractive index, slightly more than 1 for air. For radio waves in the ionosphere, $n$ behaves unusually: the carrier wave appears to travel faster than $c$ while the information it carries travels slower. Module 7 derives this and shows how receivers turn it to their advantage.

## Misconceptions
> **Misconception.** "100 ns of clock error means 30 m of position error." This mixes up a range error on one measurement with an error in the final position. How a range error becomes a position error depends on whether it is shared by every satellite and on the satellites' geometry. That is the subject of Module 2.

> **Misconception.** "The speed of light is just a very good measurement." Its value is exact by definition; what laboratories measure is time, and lengths follow from it.
===QUESTIONS===
Q1 [explain] In two or three sentences, explain why GPS is fundamentally a timing system, using $d=c\,t$.
Q2 [calculate] A satellite near your horizon is about 25,000 km away. What is the signal's travel time in milliseconds, and how much longer is it than the overhead case in Example 2?
Q3 [apply] Between corrections, a receiver's clock runs freely on its own oscillator. If the clock's contribution to range error must stay below 3 m over 30 s, what is the largest fractional frequency error $y$ the oscillator may have? Would a phone's quartz oscillator, at about $2\times10^{-6}$, meet this on its own?
Q4 [predict] Your phone's clock is 1 μs fast, and the same error affects the measured travel time from every satellite equally. Does your computed position come out about 300 m wrong? Reason it through; no maths needed.
===KEY===
Q1: The receiver measures how long each signal took to arrive; multiplying by the fixed constant c turns each time into a distance (a range), and the position follows from several ranges. Because c is fixed and huge, ranging accuracy is set by timing accuracy: about 0.3 m per nanosecond.
Q2: t = 2.5e7 m / 3.0e8 m/s ≈ 0.083 s ≈ 83 ms. The overhead case (20,200 km) is ≈ 67 ms, so about 16 ms longer.
Q3: From Δd = c·y·T ≤ 3 m with T = 30 s: y ≤ 3 / (3.0e8 × 30) ≈ 3.3e-10. A 2e-6 quartz oscillator is about 6,000 times worse (about 18 km of range error in 30 s), so the phone cannot hold time on its own and must estimate its clock offset afresh from the satellites at every fix.
Q4: No. A clock error common to every measurement shifts every range by the same ~300 m. With at least four satellites the receiver treats its own clock error as a fourth unknown and solves for it, so the common error is absorbed into the clock estimate and the position is essentially unaffected. Only errors that differ between satellites distort the position.
